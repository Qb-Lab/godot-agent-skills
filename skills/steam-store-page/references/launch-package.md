# The launch package — publishing the page is the event

Tier: **BEST PRACTICE** throughout, except where marked.

Publishing a store page is the first time the game exists publicly. Treated as a filing task,
it generates nothing; treated as an announcement, it is the one moment before release when a
wishlist push is free. And wishlists compound — they accumulate for months and convert at
launch, which is the whole argument for publishing early rather than when the game is done.

The mechanical constraint shapes the schedule `[OFFICIAL]`: the page needs Valve approval
(submit at least 7 business days ahead) and a Coming Soon page must be live at least two
weeks before release. So the announcement date is not "when we finish the page" — it is a
date you can plan backwards from.

## Prepare before the page goes live

Keep it lightweight. Five assets and one link, not a marketing campaign:

- **The store URL** — everything else points at it. Nothing ships before it resolves.
- **A concise pitch** — one sentence and one paragraph, reusable verbatim everywhere. Usually
  the short description plus one line of context.
- **The key visual** — the capsule, or a cleaner variant of it for places with no size
  constraints.
- **Two or three gameplay clips** — 5–15 seconds, no audio dependency, each showing one thing.
  The clips that work for social are usually already cut for the trailer or the About section
  demos; reuse rather than re-shoot.
- **A Steam announcement** — posted from the page itself. It reaches the people who already
  wishlisted and it is the only channel here that Steam's own algorithm sees.
- **Posts for the channels that actually exist** — Discord, the social account, the devlog.
  Writing for a channel with no audience is theatre; be honest about which ones those are.

## Outreach, proportional

Press and creator outreach at page-publish time is low-yield for an unknown game, and that is
fine — the point is the record, not the coverage. Build a small list of the people who covered
the nearest comparables (the market brief already names those games), send the pitch, the key
visual, the clips, and the URL, and move on. One short, specific email beats a press kit
nobody opens.

The exception worth real effort: a creator whose audience *is* the game's audience, where one
video moves more wishlists than every other channel combined. The market brief's Twitch and
YouTube section is where that name comes from.

## Keep talking after the page is up

The page is not a destination, it is a thing to send people to. Steam's own news and
discussion features exist for this and cost nothing: a post per milestone, and a reply habit
in the discussions. A page with no activity reads as abandoned to anyone who checks.

Do not build a website to satisfy a checklist. If the store page is the best page the game
has, a thin website actively splits attention — say so rather than adding a row to a plan.

## What goes in the plan

Record in `STORE.md` under the links and developer presence section: the announcement date,
the channels that will actually be used, who owns each, and the one creator or outlet worth a
personal pitch. Anything you cannot name an owner for is not a plan.
